Overview

The Question

Do lifestyle and adaptation factors significantly influence academic productivity during the semester? This project investigates that question through ten weeks of personal self-tracking — collecting real data on sleep, stress, study hours, motivation, and language difficulty alongside academic performance.

The academic calendar was used as an external enrichment source to classify weeks as regular or midterm, enabling direct behavioral comparison across different phases of academic pressure.

Hypothesis Testing

All Five Hypotheses
Were Significant

Pearson correlations, an independent t-test, and Spearman correlation were used at α = 0.05. Every hypothesis was rejected in favour of the alternative.

H1 Study hours → productivity (exam confounding effect) r = −0.85 · p = 0.002 Significant
H2 More sleep → higher productivity r = +0.88 · p = 0.001 Significant
H3 Higher stress → lower productivity r = −0.96 · p < 0.001 Significant
H4 Midterm weeks significantly lower productivity t = 4.79 · p = 0.001 Significant
H5 Higher motivation → higher productivity ρ = 0.74 · p = 0.015 Significant

A notable finding: H1 showed a significant negative correlation between study hours and productivity. This is explained by the exam-week confounding effect — study hours spike to ~25 hrs/week during midterms precisely when productivity is lowest due to stress and sleep deprivation.

Key Findings

What the Data Says

01

Stress is the dominant predictor of academic productivity — with a correlation of −0.96, it explains most of the variance in weekly performance.

02

Midterm weeks drop productivity by ~10 percentage points on average (54.4% → 44.5%), even when study hours are 2.5× higher.

03

More studying ≠ more productivity. Exam week study effort shows diminishing returns under high stress and sleep deprivation.

04

Behavioral variables collectively explain 92.8% of productivity variance — a strong result for a self-tracking single-subject study.

05

Simpler models win. Linear Regression outperforms ensemble methods, confirming that small datasets favor interpretable approaches.

06

Language difficulty is the second most important feature, reflecting the cognitive burden of studying in a second language throughout the semester.
